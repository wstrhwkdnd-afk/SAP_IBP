Beyond the Calendar Grid: Why IBP Time Profiles Need a Rethink

SAP IBP ships a time profile admin that works for textbook scenarios. Most real-world planning calendars are not textbook scenarios.

Anurag Rai October 2026 8 min read

Every SAP IBP implementation begins with the same foundational question: how does your organization measure time? The answer shapes every planning view, every aggregation, every report. Get it right and the system fades into the background. Get it wrong and planners spend their careers reconciling numbers that never quite agree.

The time profile is deceptively simple. It defines the periods at which demand, supply, and financial data are planned, stored, and aggregated. Day, week, month, quarter, year — straightforward hierarchies that most enterprise systems have handled for decades.

The trouble is that "week" does not mean the same thing to everyone.

What IBP gives you out of the box

SAP IBP's standard time profile administration (accessible through the Time Profile Admin app in the IBP Fiori launchpad) allows you to define planning levels and their hierarchical relationships. The system supports the standard granularities — Day, Week, Month, Quarter, and Year — and lets you configure fiscal year variants to align with your financial calendar.

For a company whose fiscal year follows the calendar year and whose weeks start on Monday, this works well. You configure the levels, set the horizon, and the system generates the periods. The admin interface handles the mapping between levels, ensuring that days roll up into weeks, weeks have a parent month, and months aggregate into quarters.

The limitations surface the moment you step outside this narrow corridor.

Where the standard approach falls short

1. Custom week start days

A significant number of organizations do not operate on a Monday-through-Sunday week. Retailers often plan Friday to Thursday to capture full weekends in a single period. Companies operating in the Middle East and parts of Asia use a Saturday or Sunday start. The IBP standard time profile, when generating weekly periods, defaults to ISO week conventions — Monday start, ISO week numbering.

If your planning week starts on Friday, you cannot simply toggle a setting. The periods must be manually created or loaded via a flat file, with each week's start and end date explicitly defined for the entire planning horizon. For a five-year horizon, that is roughly 260 weekly periods, each requiring a correct start date, end date, period ID, and description — built by hand or by a macro.

2. Technical weeks that respect month boundaries

This is the requirement that causes the most pain. In financial planning, weeks that cross month boundaries create reconciliation nightmares. If a standard week runs from Monday, March 29 to Sunday, April 4, where do those seven days of revenue belong? March or April?

A week that bleeds across a month boundary forces a choice: attribute the entire week to one month and distort that month's figures, or split the week and lose clean weekly aggregation.

The solution is what is commonly called a technical week (or split week). When a seven-day planning week would cross a month boundary, it splits into two sub-periods:

TW18a covers April 26–30 (5 days), and TW18b covers May 1 (1 day). Together they represent one logical week, but each part falls cleanly within its calendar month. Financial aggregation works. Weekly aggregation, by summing a and b parts sharing the same number, also works.

IBP does not generate these splits automatically. There is no configuration flag for "split weeks at month boundaries." Each sub-period must be defined explicitly in the time profile flat file.

3. Fiscal calendar alignment

Many organizations operate on fiscal years that do not begin in January. A fiscal year starting in April (common in India, the UK, and Japan), July (common in Australia), or October (US federal government) means that quarterly periods, annual aggregations, and year-over-year comparisons all shift. The quarter numbering must restart at the fiscal year boundary, not at the calendar year boundary.

IBP supports fiscal year variants, but configuring a time profile where quarters, technical weeks, and fiscal years all align correctly — especially when combined with a custom week start day — requires careful coordination. The interdependencies are non-trivial: a fiscal Q1 that starts in April must contain the correct technical weeks (themselves split at April 30 / May 1 if they cross that boundary), and those weeks must aggregate properly into April, May, and June at the monthly level.

4. First and last week handling

When a planning horizon starts on a Wednesday but weeks begin on Friday, the first period is a partial week — Wednesday through Thursday, just two days. Similarly, the last week of the horizon may be incomplete. These partial weeks need correct period IDs and descriptions (typically marked with a b suffix, as in TW1b) to signal to planners that the period is shorter than usual and should be treated differently in capacity and demand calculations.

IBP's standard generation does not handle this. The flat file must contain these partial periods with their correct day counts.

The real cost of getting this wrong

A misconfigured time profile does not produce an error message. It produces numbers that are almost right.

Demand planners notice that weekly forecasts do not sum to the monthly figure. Supply planners find that a week's capacity appears lower than expected, because a 5-day technical week is being measured against a 7-day standard. Financial controllers flag revenue variances that vanish when someone manually adjusts the period boundaries in a spreadsheet.

These are not bugs anyone files a ticket for. They are slow leaks of trust in the system, the kind that cause planners to maintain parallel spreadsheets "just to be safe" — exactly the behavior an IBP implementation is meant to eliminate.

How it works

You provide three inputs: a start date, an end date, and a week start day. The generator produces six complete period hierarchies:

  1. Day — every calendar day in the range, each with a unique period ID starting at 10001.
  2. Technical Week — seven-day periods that split at month boundaries. The first partial week is labeled TW1b; subsequent splits produce TWna (days in the ending month) and TWnb (days in the starting month). Numbering resets annually. Day counts are included, so planners know immediately when a period is shorter than seven days.
  3. Week — standard seven-day periods without month-boundary splitting. Useful for operational planning where financial reconciliation at the monthly level is handled separately.
  4. Month — calendar months from the first to the last day of each month within the range.
  5. Quarter — three-month periods aligned to the fiscal year. If your start date is April 1, Q1 runs April through June. Numbering resets at each fiscal year boundary.
  6. Fiscal Year — twelve-month periods from the start month through the end of that twelve-month cycle. An April start gives you FY 2024 running April 2024 through March 2025.

The output is a flat CSV with the exact column structure IBP expects for a time profile upload: Level, Period ID, Start Date, End Date, Description, and Day Count. No reformatting, no manual ID assignment, no reconciliation against a separate calendar document.

The fiscal calendar advantage

The key design decision is that every period type anchors to the start date, not to the calendar year. This is what makes fiscal calendar support work without a separate configuration step.

When your start date is July 1, 2024:

  • Quarter 1 runs July 1 – September 30, 2024
  • Quarter 2 runs October 1 – December 31, 2024
  • Quarter 3 runs January 1 – March 31, 2025
  • Quarter 4 runs April 1 – June 30, 2025
  • Fiscal Year 2024 runs July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025

Quarterly numbering resets every July. Technical weeks split at every month boundary within the fiscal year, regardless of where that boundary falls relative to the calendar year. The entire hierarchy is self-consistent: days aggregate into technical weeks, technical weeks fall within months, months compose quarters, and quarters compose fiscal years.

Implementation note

When uploading the generated CSV into IBP, map the Level column to the appropriate planning level in your time profile configuration. Level 1 (Day) maps to your daily level, Level 2 (Technical Week) to your weekly operational level, Level 4 (Month) to your monthly tactical level, and so on. The period IDs are designed with level-based prefixes (10000s for days, 20000s for technical weeks, etc.) to prevent collisions across levels.

Who needs this

Not every IBP implementation requires custom time profiles. If your company operates on calendar years with Monday-start ISO weeks and does not need weekly-to-monthly financial reconciliation, the standard time profile admin is sufficient.

The generator becomes essential when any of these conditions apply:

  • Retail and consumer goods companies planning on a Friday-to-Thursday week to keep weekends within a single planning period
  • Middle Eastern operations using a Saturday or Sunday week start to align with regional business calendars
  • Financial planning integration where weekly demand or supply figures must reconcile cleanly to monthly P&L without manual adjustments
  • Non-January fiscal years where quarter and year boundaries do not align with the calendar and where planning periods must reflect the fiscal structure, not the calendar structure
  • Multi-year planning horizons (5, 7, or 10 years) where manually maintaining a time profile flat file becomes untenable

For a 7-year horizon with all six levels, the generator produces roughly 3,500 period records in under a second. Building that same dataset by hand, with correct splits, correct IDs, and correct fiscal alignment, is a project measured in days, and every change to the horizon or week start day means starting over.

The broader point

Time profiles sit in the foundation layer of IBP. They are configured once and rarely revisited — which is precisely why getting them right at the outset matters so much. A five-minute configuration error in the time profile propagates silently into every planning view, every key figure aggregation, and every cross-functional report for the life of the system.

The standard IBP tooling assumes a world where weeks are ISO weeks, fiscal years are calendar years, and monthly reconciliation is someone else's problem. That world is smaller than the one most supply chain organizations actually operate in.

Building the time profile externally, with explicit control over week alignment, month-boundary behavior, and fiscal calendar structure, is not a workaround. It is the more reliable approach — one that encodes your organization's actual planning calendar into the system rather than hoping the system's defaults happen to match it.
