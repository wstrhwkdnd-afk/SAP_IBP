The UOM Conundrum: Planning Across Incompatible Units of Measure in IBP

When kilograms can't talk to liters and pieces refuse to become pallets, how do planning teams make sense of aggregate demand?

AR
Anurag Rai IBP Technical Consultant · October 2026

Every supply chain planner has faced this moment: leadership wants a single view of total demand across the portfolio. The sales team reports in cases. Manufacturing plans in kilograms. Procurement thinks in liters. Logistics counts pallets. And somewhere in the middle sits SAP IBP, trying to reconcile numbers that don't share a common language.

This is the Unit of Measure conundrum — one of the most persistent and quietly frustrating challenges in Integrated Business Planning. It sounds like a data mapping exercise until you realize that some conversions are genuinely impossible, and forcing them produces numbers that mislead more than they inform.

The Problem, in Plain Terms

In IBP, every material carries a base unit of measure (base UOM). A bottle of shampoo might be measured in EA (each). A bag of cement in KG. A drum of industrial solvent in L. These are the atomic units the system uses to store quantities internally.

Planning, though, doesn't happen at the individual material level alone. Demand planners need to see totals by product family. Supply planners aggregate across production lines. S&OP reviews roll everything up to the category or business unit level. And every one of those aggregations requires a common unit.

The core tension

You cannot meaningfully add 500 EA of electronic sensors to 2,000 KG of polymer resin to 300 L of coating fluid. The sum is numerically possible but semantically meaningless.

For materials within the same family — say, different sizes of the same beverage — a conversion factor often exists. A 500ml bottle and a 1L bottle can both be expressed in liters. But across product families, across categories, across divisions? The conversions either don't exist or require assumptions so broad they become dangerous.

Why Standard UOM Conversion Falls Short

SAP's UOM conversion framework is well-engineered. It supports conversion at multiple levels:

Conversion Level Scope Example
Global All materials 1 KG = 1000 G
Material-specific Single material 1 CS (case) = 24 EA for SKU-4021
Batch/lot-specific Per production run Density-dependent volume-to-weight

These work beautifully for intra-dimensional conversions — weight to weight, volume to volume, count to count. They even handle some cross-dimensional cases when a material's density or piece-weight is known and stable.

But the system cannot invent a conversion that doesn't physically exist. There is no universal constant that relates one electronic circuit board to a certain number of kilograms of lubricant. Any conversion between them requires a mediating dimension — and that's where the real design decisions begin.

The Three Incompatibility Patterns

In our experience across IBP implementations, the UOM problem shows up in three distinct patterns, each requiring a different response:

Pattern 1: Same dimension, missing conversion factors

All materials could be expressed in the same unit, but the conversion factors haven't been maintained. A product group where everything is sold by weight, but half the materials only have EA as their base UOM and nobody has recorded the piece-weight. This is a data governance problem, not a planning design problem. The fix is upstream: maintain the conversion factors in master data.

Pattern 2: Cross-dimensional but convertible

Materials span different dimensions (weight, volume, count), but a meaningful physical relationship exists. Chemical products sold by volume but produced by weight, where density is known. The conversion is real but requires material-specific factors and sometimes varies by batch or temperature. IBP can handle this with material-level UOM conversions, though the maintenance overhead is non-trivial.

Pattern 3: Genuinely incompatible

A diversified manufacturer planning diesel engines alongside upholstery fabric alongside electronic control modules. No physical conversion exists. Any shared unit would be arbitrary. This is the true conundrum, and it's far more common than it might seem — nearly every company with more than one product line hits it.

How We've Handled It in IBP

There is no single silver bullet. What works is a layered strategy that matches the approach to the aggregation level, and accepts that some aggregations simply shouldn't be done in quantity terms. Here are the approaches we've deployed across implementations:

1

Revenue as the universal translator

When physical units can't be summed, currency can. By maintaining a planning price per material, every quantity converts to revenue. IBP's key figure design supports storing both a quantity-based plan and a value-based plan, and S&OP dashboards aggregate on value. This is the most common solution for executive-level roll-ups. The trade-off: it hides volume shifts behind price movements. A 10% drop in volume masked by a 12% price increase looks like growth in revenue terms.

2

Statistical units at the planning level

Define a planning UOM that is distinct from the base UOM — a normalized "statistical case" or "standard unit" that each product family agrees on. For instance, a beverages division might define 1 SU = 1 liter equivalent, and assign conversion factors: a 24-pack of 330ml cans = 7.92 SU. This works within product families but still breaks down across fundamentally different categories. In IBP, this is implemented through an additional UOM conversion maintained at the planning level, stored as a key figure attribute.

3

Disaggregated planning with selective aggregation

Plan at the material level in each material's native UOM. Aggregate only where conversion is valid. Use IBP's planning area design to define multiple aggregation hierarchies: one for weight-based products, one for volume-based, one for discrete. At the top level, aggregate in value only. This respects the physics of the products and avoids misleading sums. The design is more complex — multiple planning views, conditional aggregation logic — but it produces numbers people can trust.

4

Proxy weight / volume factors with explicit assumptions

For Pattern 2 materials (cross-dimensional but convertible), maintain a QUANTITY_WEIGHT or QUANTITY_VOLUME key figure at the product level. A calculated key figure then derives the aggregated quantity: CONSENSUS_QTY_KG = CONSENSUS_QTY * QUANTITY_WEIGHT. The conversion factor is visible, auditable, and owned by a specific team. When density or piece-weight changes, the factor updates — and the plan recalculates automatically through IBP's calculation engine.

5

Accept the boundary and design around it

Sometimes the right answer is to not aggregate. Instead of forcing 50 product families into one number, design IBP dashboards that show each family in its own unit with its own trend line, side by side. Comparative growth rates (percentage change) can be shown together even when absolute quantities can't. A well-designed analytics story in IBP can give leadership the portfolio view they need without requiring a single summed number that nobody can defend.

The Implementation Playbook

In practice, we've found that the UOM strategy needs to be decided early in the IBP design phase, not discovered during integration testing when the numbers don't add up. Here's the sequence that has worked:

Step 1: Classify every planning product into a UOM compatibility group

Before configuring a single planning area, map each product family to one of the three patterns above. This classification becomes a master data attribute and drives downstream design decisions.

Step 2: Define the "planning UOM" governance model

Who owns the conversion factors? How often are they reviewed? What happens when a new product launches without one? In IBP, missing conversion factors don't throw errors — they produce zeros or blanks at aggregate levels, which are worse than errors because they go unnoticed. Build validation checks into your planning process, not just your data model.

Step 3: Design key figures with dual-UOM awareness

For every quantity key figure, decide whether it needs a parallel value key figure. Structure calculated key figures so that the UOM conversion is explicit and traceable. Avoid burying conversions inside complex nested formulas where a wrong factor becomes invisible.

A common mistake

Teams often try to solve this entirely through IBP's UOM conversion configuration, treating it as a technical setup task. But UOM compatibility is a planning process design decision. The technology supports whatever you decide — the question is what you decide, and whether the business understands the assumptions behind the numbers they see.

Step 4: Configure IBP planning views accordingly

Create separate planning views for quantity planning (in native UOM) and value planning (in currency). Use IBP's filter and attribute-based navigation to let planners move between views without losing context. At aggregate levels, default the view to value — and make the quantity view available only where the UOM is homogeneous.

What About the "Standard Pallet" Approach?

Some teams attempt a logistics-unit normalization: express everything in pallets, containers, or truckloads. The logic is appealing — if the supply chain ultimately moves physical objects on physical vehicles, why not plan in those terms?

It works for logistics and distribution planning, where the pallet is the unit of work. But it's a poor fit for demand planning or S&OP aggregation. Customer demand doesn't arrive in pallets. Marketing campaigns target cases or units. Finance reports in currency. Forcing everything through a logistics lens distorts the demand signal at the very level where accuracy matters most.

The right move is to use logistics units in the logistics planning layer of IBP and let each planning stage speak its own language, with clean conversion points between them.

The Honest Summary

The UOM conundrum doesn't have a clean resolution. It has a set of trade-offs that each organization navigates based on their product mix, their planning maturity, and how much ambiguity their leadership is willing to see in a dashboard.

The worst outcome is a system that appears to aggregate correctly — where the numbers add up but the sum means nothing. The best outcome is a system that aggregates where it can, converts where the physics allow, reports in value where it must, and is honest about where the boundaries are.

IBP gives you the tools. The hard part, as always, is the design decisions that come before the configuration.
